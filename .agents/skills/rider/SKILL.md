Use this progression through the supplied primer:

1. **The basic design and wave degree** — book pages 7–13: five-wave progress, three-wave or complex correction, nested structure, degree labels.
2. **Motive waves** — pages 14–18: impulse rules, extensions, truncations, and diagonal triangles.
3. **Corrective waves** — pages 19–25: zigzags, flats, triangles, and combinations; identify subdivisions before assigning a pattern name.
4. **Guidelines** — pages 26–28: alternation, correction depth, channels, and volume observations. Guidelines help compare valid counts; they do not override rules.
5. **Count selection and uncertainty** — pages 29–31: deductive elimination, competing valid interpretations, and waiting when the evidence does not distinguish them.
6. **Fibonacci and ratio analysis** — pages 32–39: calculate relationships from named pivots, treat ratios as guidelines, and avoid presenting a ratio match as proof.
7. **Perspective and terminology** — pages 40–42: review the author's thesis and the glossary. Separate the author's claims about markets and forecasting from independently measured evidence.

## Step-by-step multi-timeframe analysis

Always work from the highest requested timeframe down to the lowest requested timeframe. Complete and document each timeframe pass before using the next lower timeframe. Lower-timeframe detail may refine or challenge the current working interpretation, but it must never silently replace the higher-timeframe structure. Timeframe does not by itself determine wave degree.

1. **Define the assignment and evidence boundary.** Record the instrument and actual contract if known, chart/data source, every requested timeframe, cutoff and timezone, price scale, session/roll convention, and whether the last bar is complete. State which inputs are missing, estimated from pixels, or otherwise uncertain. Do not fill gaps with invented market data.
2. **Order the charts.** Sort the supplied timeframes from highest to lowest (for example, Weekly → Daily → 4H → 1H). Note gaps in the sequence. The highest chart establishes the initial parent context; do not assume a fixed degree name from its timeframe. Inspect every supplied chart for context, but produce only the timeframe deliverables the user requested.
3. **Read the highest timeframe first.** Describe the visible trend and major swings without labels. Mark candidate pivots from the supplied source, distinguish confirmed/closed-bar pivots from developing ones, and record the source locations or approximate prices and times with their precision limits. Check that timestamp spacing and bar count agree with the chart's stated timeframe; if they conflict, gate or qualify any analysis that depends on the interval.
4. **Set the parent structure and plausible degree.** Propose the simplest degree and parent path that fit the visible history. Separate observed completed structures from incomplete possibilities. If several parent interpretations remain valid, keep them explicit rather than selecting one through confidence alone.
5. **Build and audit the higher-timeframe candidates.** For each supported candidate, identify motive versus corrective mode and proposed subdivisions. Apply hard rules first; then use relevant guidelines to rank candidates. Record rule failures, supporting and conflicting evidence, invalidation, and the observation that would distinguish the candidates. Preserve the strongest material alternate.
6. **Freeze the working higher-timeframe context for refinement.** Carry its method, degree, parent path, pivots, valid alternatives, and unresolved parts into the next timeframe. Treat a forecast or incomplete wave as conditional, not as an observed pivot. If the high-timeframe evidence is too weak to support a parent context, mark that uncertainty before continuing.
7. **Move down exactly one supplied timeframe.** First relate its swings to the higher-timeframe parent legs. Then identify its own candidate pivots and possible subdivisions. Ask whether these details fit inside the parent path and degree already proposed. Use the lower chart to refine internal structure and timing, not to erase a valid higher-timeframe structure merely because the lower chart has a more visually compelling count.
8. **Audit fit and conflicts.** Check whether the lower-timeframe count nests consistently within each viable higher-timeframe candidate, respects the same method's rules, and uses coherent degrees. If it conflicts, name the exact pivot or rule conflict. Revisit the parent only when the supplied evidence genuinely invalidates it; show the reason and preserve the prior interpretation as a revision. Do not force alignment by relabeling pivots or changing methods mid-count. Give each material pivot a stable identity across timeframe views where the same swing is represented.
9. **Carry alternatives downward.** Where two higher-timeframe counts remain materially viable, test the lower-timeframe evidence against both. State whether it supports one, supports neither, or does not yet distinguish them. Do not discard an alternate solely because it is inconvenient to refine.
10. **Repeat through every requested timeframe.** Continue one level at a time until the lowest requested timeframe has been examined. At each level retain its parent link, degree, source/cutoff, observed pivots, candidate subdivisions, rule audit, uncertainty, and invalidation. Never start from the lowest chart and construct a preferred count upward.
11. **Synthesize the full hierarchy.** Check that each lower-timeframe count fits its parent path and that the complete chart set tells a consistent nested story. Separate genuine contradictions from unresolved subdivisions or incomplete bars. If no coherent hierarchy is supported, report the conflict and stop short of a forced count.
12. **Forecast the next count.** Always include a forecast of the next plausible wave sequence in future chart space and explain the pivots, parent count, rule checks, and guidelines behind it. State the confirmation condition, invalidation, and horizon only when supported. If evidence does not select one path, show the materially plausible conditional branches and say that the paths are not distinguished; do not invent subdivisions or omit the forecast merely because confidence is low. Bind every trigger, projection, and invalidation to the correct source bar and value, verify the condition against that bar's range, and keep a historical wave pivot distinct from a later invalidation threshold. A wave forecast is a structural hypothesis, not a promise or a trade instruction.
13. **Report and preserve the analysis state.** Lead with `supported` or `provisional`; state the affected degree and the exact structure or rule check that is not established. Present the timeframe hierarchy from highest to lowest, cite the data/chart cutoff and method, show the rule audit and uncertainty, and distinguish historical labels from forecasts and alternatives. If the analysis is being recorded, retain its original cutoff, count version, and invalidations so a later review cannot rewrite what was known.

If any step lacks evidence needed to continue, say which field or structure is unresolved and continue only with the parts that remain supportable. Do not manufacture a lower-timeframe refinement to make the hierarchy look complete.

### Count labels and hierarchy

Apply this three-degree style consistently on every requested chart. A chart is not complete merely because all three styles appear near the latest price: the three layers must be applied to the supported historical count from the oldest visible, defensible pivot through the current structure. Each timeframe gets its own historical count and its own completeness check.

| Count degree | Label appearance | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Highest displayed degree | Light neutral/charcoal, double parentheses | `((I))`, `((II))`, `((III))`, `((IV))`, `((V))`; corrective `((A))`, `((B))`, `((C))` |
| Middle displayed degree | Blue, single parentheses | `(i)`, `(ii)`, `(iii)`, `(iv)`, `(v)`; corrective `(a)`, `(b)`, `(c)` |
| Inner displayed degree | Red, unparenthesized | `i`, `ii`, `iii`, `iv`, `v`; corrective `a`, `b`, `c` |

Use uppercase `A/B/C` for the main legs of a corrective pattern and lowercase `a/b/c` only for lower-degree corrective subdivisions; the chosen degree still controls color and parentheses. Keep the same notation convention throughout the chart. Put each label at its actual pivot. Work left-to-right and label historical pivots across the visible swing sequence; do not concentrate counts only around the latest candles. When multiple degrees share a pivot, stack their labels together at that same time/price anchor in the order highest, middle, inner; use small vertical offsets or leader lines only for legibility, without implying separate pivots. Mark an uncertain label with `?` while preserving its degree's required color and parentheses. Do not make up a fourth color or bracket style: if more detail is necessary, use a separate synchronized inset/panel with the same three-degree grammar and an explicit parent link.

For Quant Lab's chart-markup JSON, put the **bare wave symbol** (`I`, `ii`, `c`) in `wave` and declare its `degree`; the renderer adds the parentheses and uncertainty mark. ### Trendlines, channels, and breaks

Use these chart-structure practices alongside the traditional Frost–Prechter count; they do not create or validate a wave count by themselves. Keep Elliott channeling guidelines distinct from generic price channels. The supplied primer introduces channels as guidelines (pages 26–28). For precise anchors, consult Elliott Wave International’s [Channeling guide](https://www.elliottwave.com/waveopedia/channeling/), which summarizes the traditional Frost–Prechter technique; its market-performance and promotional claims are not treated as independent evidence. For general trendline anchor selection and break interpretation, see the CMT Association’s [trendline drawing guide](https://cmtassociation.org/technically_speaking/technically-speaking-may-2017/); apply its peak/trough and close-confirmation discussion without importing its trading examples as Rider rules.

- Identify pivots from the supplied chart before drawing. A line through two confirmed pivots is a **candidate** trendline; prefer an independent third touch before calling it **supported**. More touches can strengthen the description but do not prove a future hold. State the number/quality of visible touches and avoid false precision when anchors come from screenshot pixels.
- Draw rising support through confirmed higher lows and falling resistance through confirmed lower highs. Use actual visible swing wicks or pivot prices consistently, do not force a line through unrelated candles, and extend beyond the latest bar only as a conditional guide. A touch or wick overshoot alone is a test, not a confirmed break.
- Build a generic price channel from a defensible trendline plus a parallel rail anchored to the opposing swing(s). Name the pivots used for both rails. If a parallel fit requires arbitrary offsets, has too few independent touches, or distorts the visible structure, label it **candidate channel** or leave it off. Do not imply that an approximate screenshot channel is measured market data.
- When the working Elliott structure supplies the required pivots, use the traditional channeling guideline: after wave 3, connect the ends of waves 1 and 3 and draw a parallel through wave 2 to estimate wave 4’s boundary. After wave 4, connect waves 2 and 4; use a parallel through wave 3 to estimate wave 5 when waves 1 and 3 are proportionate. If wave 3 is unusually strong, compare a parallel through wave 1. For a zigzag correction, connect the start of A to the end of B, then draw a parallel through the end of A; compare it with the A–C line. Apply only when the count and pivots support it; do not force the count to fit the channel.
- Distinguish a **channel test**, a **wick beyond a rail**, and a **confirmed break**. Require a completed bar beyond the stated boundary for a close-confirmed break; if the last bar is forming, say the break is intrabar/provisional. Note whether price closes back inside, retests, or holds beyond the line only when the supplied bars show it.
- Before drawing on a screenshot, identify each line’s source pivot and anchor it to the actual visible wick tip in native image coordinates. If OHLC data is available, use the exact pivot price/time; otherwise describe the pixel anchor as approximate. Mark anchor points so the viewer can verify them. Do not draw a line through a candle body or unrelated wick to make a fit look cleaner: re-anchor to defensible pivots, stop the line at its first valid break, or omit it. A trendline may cross later bars only where those bars are explicitly being shown as tests/breaks, which must be marked and explained.
- Build parallel rails from a defensible slope and a named opposing wick pivot, not an arbitrary vertical offset. Report the touch count and quality. Keep a weak or two-touch fit labeled `candidate`; stop the channel at a visible break/undercut and mark the breaking wick. Do not extend rails through post-break candles as if the channel remains intact.
- Match the supplied chart-technician reference: use thin, light neutral dashed/dotted channel rails and red dotted lines for specifically identified breaks, undercuts, or decision boundaries. Add a compact legend and name each structure (for example, `candidate falling channel` or `break test`). Color is never the only cue. Keep the bars and anchor wicks visible; route labels and callouts into open chart space.
- For break interpretation, distinguish an intrabar wick through a line from a completed-bar close beyond it. State the timeframe and completion status. A red dotted line is a visual boundary, not an entry signal or proof of reversal.
- Put anchors, touch count, whether the latest bar is complete, and the exact observation that would upgrade or reject the line in the floating findings summary. Keep projected channel paths conditional; never turn a channel break into an entry, order, target, or trading instruction.

Apply hard rules before using guidelines. For a traditional impulse, check at minimum:

- Wave 2 does not move beyond the origin of wave 1.
- Wave 3 moves beyond the end of wave 1 and is not the shortest of waves 1, 3, and 5.
- Wave 4 does not overlap wave 1 in an impulse. Consider a diagonal only where the method permits it and its own structure supports it; do not use the diagonal exception to excuse a broken impulse.

If a hard rule fails, reject that candidate under this method or explicitly identify the supported pattern-specific exception. Do not relabel after seeing the outcome to save a count. Guidelines such as alternation, common Fibonacci relationships, channel contact, and volume tendencies rank otherwise-valid interpretations; they are not mandatory rules and do not make a count certain.

Present one preferred count and one materially distinct alternate when the evidence supports them. For each, show:

- Method, degree, parent path, and source/timeframe/cutoff.
- Observed pivots and why the proposed subdivisions fit.
- Rule audit, then relevant supporting and conflicting guidelines.
- The exact price event that invalidates the candidate, if available from the source.
- Which next observation would distinguish the preferred count from the alternate.
- Conditional projections in future chart space only, with the projection basis and horizon when supported.

Do not promote subdivisions that are not supported. If no candidate is convincingly preferred or input quality cannot support the count, say **developing / structure not yet established** and name the missing test. Do not invent a more detailed count merely to fill every label. Keep forecast labels visually distinct from historical labels and any alternate path.

## Fibonacci and measurement

For every chart count, look for a suitable, chart-visible swing and include its Fibonacci retracement unless the anchors or scale do not support a reliable measurement. Name the start and end pivots, mark the swing high and low in blue, and record their prices and timeframe, measurement direction, price units, and calculation. For an advance from low `L` to high `H`, a downward retracement level is `H − r × (H − L)`. For a decline from high `H` to low `L`, an upward retracement level is `L + r × (H − L)`. On the chart, emphasize only the three active pullback levels—normally 38.2%, 50%, and 61.8%—in green, yellow, and red respectively, each at twice the baseline guide width. Do not draw other Fib lines unless they are in play for the selected count; additional ratios may be stated in the ledger when useful without being drawn. State the raw calculation before rounding to displayed price precision. Keep screenshot-estimated anchors and resulting levels marked approximate. State current price's relation to relevant Fib levels only as a chart observation, not confirmation of a wave. Separate retracement from extension and arithmetic price distance from percentage change where relevant.
